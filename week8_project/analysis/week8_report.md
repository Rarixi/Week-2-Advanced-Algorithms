# Week 8 Report Computational Complexity and Approximation

## Executive Summary

This week was about hitting a wall on purpose and then figuring out what to do once you hit it. Every algorithm up to this point in the course has had an exact answer and a reasonable amount of time to find it in. This week's problems, vertex cover, the traveling salesman problem, satisfiability, subset sum, do not offer that combination, no known algorithm solves any of them exactly in polynomial time, and the backtracking and brute force solvers I built to find exact answers anyway prove the point by blowing up exponentially right in front of me. The two tools for actually doing something useful here are reductions, which let hardness carry over from one problem to another so you only have to prove a problem is hard once, and approximation algorithms, which give up the exact answer on purpose in exchange for a provable bound on how wrong they are allowed to be, running in polynomial time instead of exponential. My own numbers backed both ideas up directly, the 3 SAT to vertex cover reduction agreed with an independent SAT solver on every instance I threw at it, and the greedy vertex cover approximation never once broke its promised 2x bound even though it got close.

## Methodology

I timed everything with perf counter, and for the exponential algorithms I didn't just time one random instance per size, since a lucky random instance can finish fast no matter how big n is, which would hide the exponential growth rather than show it. For subset sum I used the genuine worst case, a target set to one more than the sum of all the numbers, so no subset can ever reach it and the backtracking search has nothing to prune on, forcing the full search tree every single time, at sizes 10 through 22 items. For the SAT solver I generated random 3 CNF formulas at a clauses to variables ratio of about 4.3, the ratio that's empirically known to produce the hardest instances for random 3 SAT, and averaged 8 trials per size at sizes 10 through 26 variables, since even at the hard ratio individual instances still vary a lot in how fast they resolve. For vertex cover and TSP I split the benchmark in two. The approximation ratio sweep stayed small, 6 to 18 vertices for vertex cover and 5 to 10 cities for TSP, since getting the true optimal answer to compare against meant running my own brute force exact solvers, which are themselves exponential, a handful of trials at each size. The runtime sweep went much bigger, up to 800 vertices for vertex cover and 1000 cities for TSP, since the approximation algorithms alone are polynomial and have nothing exponential holding them back. For the reduction, I validated correctness on two hand built instances, one satisfiable and one not, plus 8 random 3 CNF formulas, checking that a target size vertex cover existing in the constructed graph matched whether the original formula was actually satisfiable, confirmed independently through my own SAT solver and an exact brute force vertex cover solver rather than just trusting the construction on faith.

## Results

Subset sum backtracking ran in about 287 microseconds at 10 items, climbing to roughly 1391178 microseconds, just under a second and a half, at 22 items, a growth factor of almost 4850 times for only 12 more items added to the list. The greedy heuristic barely moved the whole time, staying in the 3 to 8 microsecond range regardless of n, since its cost comes entirely from sorting, not from exploring a tree that doubles with every extra item. The SAT solver showed the same shape, about 563 microseconds at 10 variables climbing to roughly 574688 microseconds at 26 variables, a growth factor around 1021 times for 16 more variables, with the mean number of search nodes visited climbing right alongside it, from about 110 up to about 37042.

Vertex cover's greedy approximation stayed comfortably inside its promised 2x bound across every single trial I ran, averaging a ratio of about 1.474 across 28 trials spanning 6 to 18 vertices, meaning the greedy cover came in roughly 47 percent larger than the true optimal on average. The worst single trial hit exactly 2.0, right at the theoretical ceiling, which happened at the smallest size tested, 6 vertices, where one unlucky edge pick can double the cover outright on a small graph. TSP's nearest neighbor heuristic did noticeably better relative to its own worst case, averaging a ratio of about 1.087 across 30 trials spanning 5 to 10 cities, with the worst single trial landing around 1.30 at 10 cities. The TSP ratio also showed a mild upward drift with city count, from an average of about 1.026 at 5 cities up to about 1.139 at 10 cities, suggesting nearest neighbor's early greedy choices leave more opportunities for a bad detour to compound as there are more cities left to visit. On the runtime side, both approximation algorithms stayed clearly polynomial, vertex cover's greedy approach went from about 108 microseconds at 50 vertices to about 254422 microseconds at 800 vertices, and TSP's nearest neighbor went from about 208 microseconds at 50 cities to about 83873 microseconds at 1000 cities, nowhere close to the exponential curves the exact solvers would have produced at those same sizes. The reduction held up on every single one of the 10 instances I validated it against, the two hand built cases and all 8 random formulas, the vertex cover solver's answer on whether a target size cover existed always matched the SAT solver's answer on whether the formula was satisfiable.

## Discussion

P versus NP is really asking whether being able to quickly check a proposed answer means you can also quickly find one in the first place. Every np complete problem this week shares that property, verifying a satisfying assignment, a small vertex cover, or a short tour is fast, but no one has ever found an algorithm that finds one of those answers from scratch in polynomial time, and my own exponential curves are exactly what that gap looks like in practice, not just in theory. Reductions matter practically because they mean you only have to prove hardness once and then every problem it reduces to inherits that proof for free, which is why the 3 SAT to vertex cover construction isn't just an exercise, it's a demonstration that an entire family of problems can borrow the same hardness result instead of needing its own separate proof. Approximation algorithms matter for the opposite reason, once you accept that an exact answer isn't coming in reasonable time, a provable bound on how wrong you're willing to be, 2x for vertex cover, a measured 1.087x on average for TSP here, turns an intractable problem back into something you can actually ship. The clearest insight from the experiments themselves was just how fast the exponential curves actually explode once I saw real numbers instead of just the O(2^n) notation, going from 287 microseconds to almost a second and a half for subset sum over a jump of only 12 items in n is the kind of growth that notation warns you about but doesn't make visceral until you watch it happen on your own machine.

## Conclusion

Coming into this week I understood P versus NP as a definition to memorize, now it feels more like a wall I ran into directly, every backtracking and brute force solver I wrote this week hit it the moment n got past the low 20s, and no amount of clever pruning changed the shape of the curve, only how soon it started climbing. Understanding that limit is what actually guides algorithm design here, once you know a problem is np complete you stop hunting for an exact polynomial algorithm that almost certainly doesn't exist and instead ask the more useful question, what's the best bound I can guarantee if I give up exactness, which is exactly the question vertex cover's 2x bound and TSP's measured 1.087x both answer. That shift, from demanding the exact answer to demanding a provably bounded answer, feels like the real throughline connecting this week back to the probabilistic tradeoffs from randomized algorithms and forward to wherever the course goes next.
