# Week 9 Report, Approximation Algorithms

This week was about problems that don't have fast exact algorithms, at least not any known ones, and what you do instead, which is settle for an algorithm that runs fast and is provably close to the true optimum, even if it's not guaranteed to hit it exactly. I built five algorithms this way, a greedy 2x approximation for Vertex Cover, a greedy heuristic for Facility Location, an LP relaxation and two rounding methods for Set Cover, an MST doubling 2x approximation for Metric TSP, and a randomized coin flip algorithm for Max Cut. For every single one of these I also wrote a brute force exact solver, not because anyone would ever use brute force in practice, but because I wanted to actually measure how close the approximations land to optimal rather than just trust the proofs and move on.

## Vertex Cover

The greedy algorithm here is about as simple as it gets. Pick any edge that isn't covered yet, throw both its endpoints into the cover, remove every edge that touches either of them, and repeat until nothing's left uncovered. The proof that this never does worse than 2x optimal relies on the edges you pick being vertex disjoint from each other by construction, which means the true optimal cover has to spend at least one vertex on each edge you picked, so optimal is at least as big as the number of edges you picked, and your cover is exactly twice that. Running this against brute force across 18 trial graphs with 5 to 10 vertices, the mean ratio came out to 1.381, nowhere close to the worst case bound of 2.0, with individual trials ranging all the way from a perfect 1.0 up to exactly 2.0 on one unlucky graph. That spread is basically the point, the 2x bound is a guarantee, not a typical outcome, and most real graphs give greedy plenty of room to land well under it.

## Facility Location

This one doesn't come with the same clean proof as Vertex Cover, the greedy heuristic just keeps opening whichever currently closed facility would cut total cost the most, until opening anything else stops helping. It's not the fancier 1.61x approximation you'd get from LP rounding a la Shmoys and Tardos, just a simple, practical greedy. And it performed really well, mean ratio of 1.005 across 15 small instances, with 13 of those 15 landing on the literal exact optimum, matched by brute force to the decimal. The worst case I saw was a ratio of 1.066, so even when it missed, it missed by less than 7 percent. This genuinely surprised me a little, I expected more daylight between greedy and optimal on at least a few instances, but apparently when clients and facilities are scattered randomly across a plane there usually isn't a cleverer combination hiding just out of greedy's reach.

## Set Cover

Set Cover got the most involved treatment. I modeled it as an integer program, relaxed the integrality constraint so each set gets a fractional weight between 0 and 1, solved that relaxed version with scipy's linprog, and then had to turn the fractional answer back into an actual choice of whole sets. I implemented two ways to do that rounding. Deterministic rounding looks at f, the largest number of sets any single element belongs to, and rounds any set with fractional weight at or above 1 over f up to a full 1. It's dead simple and always produces a valid cover in one pass, but its guarantee is an f approximation, and f can get ugly if your instance has heavily overlapping sets. Randomized rounding instead treats each set's fractional weight as a literal probability and flips a coin per set, with a cleanup pass afterward adding the cheapest set covering anything that got missed. Across small instances with 6 to 14 elements, deterministic rounding averaged a ratio of 1.379 with a rough instance spiking all the way to 3.296, while randomized rounding's mean ratio averaged out to 1.096, genuinely beating deterministic on average, and I could watch the variance in its outcomes firsthand, trial costs really did bounce around run to run on the same instance, with ratio variance as high as 0.130 on one of them. That variance is exactly why a single randomized trial doesn't tell you much and you want to run it a bunch of times, which the benchmark does, 20 trials per instance.

One thing worth calling out specifically, my first version of randomized rounding scaled every probability by ln(n), which is what the textbook analysis uses to get its formal O(log n) bound. When I actually ran it though, every trial produced the exact same cover, zero variance, because ln(n) on these small instances was big enough to push almost every nonzero probability straight up to 1, turning a randomized algorithm into a deterministic one by accident. I switched the default to an unscaled version, probability equal to the raw LP weight, which kept real randomness alive and let the variance actually show up in the data, at the cost of walking away from the formal asymptotic guarantee on instances this small. Worth knowing the textbook bound and what it actually looks like at small n can be two different things.

## Metric TSP

For Metric TSP I went with MST doubling over Christofides, since the assignment allowed either and MST doubling needs nothing more exotic than a minimum spanning tree and an Eulerian circuit, no minimum weight perfect matching required. Build the MST with Prim's algorithm, double every edge so every vertex has even degree, walk an Eulerian circuit with Hierholzer's algorithm, then shortcut past any vertex you've already visited, which the triangle inequality guarantees only ever shortens the tour, never lengthens it. Across 18 trials with 5 to 10 cities, the mean ratio was 1.102, well under the 2x bound, topping out at 1.253 on the roughest instance. I also explicitly checked the tighter intermediate claim the proof relies on, that the final tour never exceeds 2 times the raw MST weight, and that held on every single trial I ran.

## Max Cut

Max Cut's randomized algorithm is almost absurdly simple, every vertex independently flips a fair coin and that decides which side it's on. The proof that this gives an expected cut of at least half of optimal just falls out of linearity of expectation, each edge crosses with probability exactly 1 over 2 regardless of the graph's shape, and optimal can never exceed the total edge count, so you're done. I ran 30 random trials per graph across instances with 6 to 10 vertices and the overall mean random ratio landed at 0.669, comfortably above the 0.5 floor, though individual unlucky trials did dip as low as 0 cut edges on a couple of runs, which is completely consistent with the bound only holding in expectation, not on every single trial. I also ran local search on top of each random start, repeatedly flipping any vertex that had more same side neighbors than other side neighbors until no flip helped anymore, and that pushed the mean ratio up to 0.942, getting close to true optimal most of the time without ever doing worse than the random start it began from.

## Runtime and Scaling

The runtime sweeps, which skip the exact solvers entirely and just time the approximations themselves on bigger instances, showed exactly the polynomial scaling you'd hope for. Vertex Cover went from about 5 microseconds at n equals 10 up to about 29000 microseconds at n equals 320, which tracks with its roughly quadratic edge checking. The more interesting comparison was Max Cut's random assignment against local search, random stayed cheap the whole way, about 472 microseconds even at n equals 320, while local search climbed to about 14000 microseconds at that same size, since it has to keep sweeping every vertex and checking its neighbors until nothing improves anymore. That's the real cost of local search's better approximation ratio, it's doing more work per instance meaningfully to get there.

## Wrapping Up

Putting all five side by side, the clearest theme is how much daylight there usually is between a worst case approximation bound and what you actually see on typical random instances. Every single algorithm here beat its theoretical bound by a solid margin on average, Vertex Cover and TSP both sitting well under their 2x ceilings, Max Cut's random version sitting well above its 0.5 floor, and Facility Location barely missing optimal at all. The exceptions were the trials that made the bounds worth proving in the first place, the one Vertex Cover graph that hit exactly 2.0, the Set Cover instance where deterministic rounding spiked past 3x. Having the exact and brute force solvers sitting right next to every approximation made all of this something I could actually see in the numbers rather than take on faith from the proofs.
